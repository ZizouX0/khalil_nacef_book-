# Reading Spanish Out Loud

Spanish spelling is close to a phonetic transcription. Learn what each letter is worth and where the stress
falls, and the page has already told you almost everything.

Almost. Spelling hides two things: **rhythm** and **linking**. Between them they are the reason a Spaniard
talking at a perfectly ordinary speed sounds to you like someone in a hurry.

## 1 · Every syllable is the same size

English is stress-timed. You hammer the strong syllables and crush everything between them: *photography*
comes out as **f'-TOG-r'-fee**, three vowels flattened into a neutral *uh*. French is different but equally
un-Spanish — it gathers words into groups and puts one accent at the end of each group, so the last syllable
before a pause is the one that stretches.

Spanish does neither. **Every syllable gets roughly the same amount of time, and no vowel is ever reduced.**
The final *a* of *ensalada* is exactly the same *a* as the stressed one: **en-sa-LA-da**, four even beats,
four identical *a*s. One is a little stronger than the rest, but never at its neighbours' expense.

This one habit is worth more than every consonant in this book put together.

Say *en-s'-LAH-d'* and you have produced two vowels Spanish does not own, inside a word that was otherwise
perfect. Say *en-sa-la-DA* and you have moved the beat to the end, which in Spanish is how *hablo* (I speak)
turns into *habló* (he spoke). Neither mistake is exotic. Both are your first language on autopilot.

> **Do this.** Tap the table once per syllable, evenly, like a metronome, and fit the word to your taps. If a
> tap lands on nothing, you swallowed a vowel. If two taps come out different lengths, slow down.

## 2 · Words do not have edges

Spanish is written with spaces. It is not spoken with them.

Inside a breath group, a word ending in a consonant hands that consonant straight to the next word, if the
next word begins with a vowel:

- *los amigos* → **lo-sa-mi-gos**
- *el hombre* → **e-lom-bre** (the *h* was never there)
- *un año* → **u-na-ño** — which is exactly why it can sound like *una*

And when two vowels meet across a space, they get squeezed into a single beat:

- *la escuela* → **laes-cue-la** — three beats, not four
- *mi amigo* → **mia-mi-go**
- *va a hablar* → **va-blar** — three *a*s become one

Nothing is being dropped or slurred here. This is what a Spanish sentence *is*: a chain of syllables with the
word boundaries painted over. It also means *tienes alas* and *tiene salas* are genuinely identical out loud,
and only context tells them apart.

> **Do this.** Take any line of dialogue. In pencil, draw a small arc everywhere a final consonant meets a
> following vowel, and everywhere two vowels touch across a space. Then read the line as one long word.

## 3 · The protocol — run this on every dialogue

Five passes. Ten minutes. Pencil in hand.

I would run this on the dialogue only, never on every sentence in the unit. It is too slow to spend on
everything, and the dialogue is where the linking shows up anyway.

1. **Mark the beats.** Over every word of two or more syllables, put a dot on the stressed syllable. Written
   accent → the dot goes there. No accent and the word ends in a vowel, *-n* or *-s* → second-to-last
   syllable. No accent, any other ending → last syllable.
2. **Draw the chains.** Arcs for the linking, as above.
3. **Beat it out silently.** Tap once per syllable, evenly, right through the line, saying nothing. You are
   checking that you know how many beats there are before you commit your mouth to them.
4. **Say it, three times.** Once at half speed, with full short identical vowels, no puff of air on *p*, *t*
   or *c*, and every *r* a single flick unless it is *rr* or starts the word. Then once at normal speed. Then
   once slightly too fast.
5. **Record and check.** Phone, voice memo, one take. Play it back and ask three questions, and only these
   three: *Did the beat land on my pencil dots? Did any vowel turn into English "uh" or French "eu"? Did I
   stop between words?* Fix one thing, re-record once, stop.

Don't try to judge your accent from the recording. You can't — you have nothing to compare it against. Those
three questions you *can* judge, alone and reliably, because all three are decisions you made on paper before
you opened your mouth. That is the whole trick of working without audio: **only ever check yourself against
something you can see.**

## 4 · What this book cannot do

Everything above trains your mouth. None of it trains your ear, and the two are not the same skill.

Work through the **Suena así** boxes and the protocol and you will produce Spanish with the right vowels, the
right stress, the right even rhythm, the right joins. That really is most of what makes a speaker
intelligible.

Here is what you will not get. You will not *hear* Spanish. The linking you have learned to produce, you will
not recognise in anyone else's speech, and fast speech will still arrive as one undifferentiated stream. You
will have no reliable idea whether your *r*, your *j* and your five vowels match a Spanish speaker's, because
you have never heard one to compare against and your own voice sounds different from the inside. You will not
get the rolled **rr** off a page; even advanced, taught learners mostly don't. And nobody will correct you.
Nothing in a book can tell you that the thing you have been confidently repeating for six months is wrong.

So two things are eventually not optional. **A lot of listening**, so your ear builds the categories your
mouth has been guessing at. And **a few hours, at least, with a person who will interrupt you** — a teacher,
a tutor, an exchange partner. Feedback is the strongest single ingredient in the research on pronunciation
teaching, and it is the one ingredient print cannot contain.

Treat this chapter as preparation for that day. Not a substitute for it.

> **About the respellings.** The **Suena así** boxes spell words out in English-friendly syllables (*la
> pizarra* → pee-THAH-rrah) with the strong beat in capitals. They are a scaffold, not the goal: Spanish
> spelling already tells you the sounds almost perfectly, and a made-up English-looking string will
> eventually teach you English habits. **By the middle of A2 you should be reading the Spanish and using the
> respelling only to check yourself. Then not needing it at all.**
>
> They use the Spanish of Spain, where **z** and **c** before *e/i* are the *th* of *think*. In Latin America
> the same letters are a plain **s**. Both are completely standard.
