# Reading Spanish Out Loud

Spanish spelling is close to a phonetic transcription. Once you know what each letter is worth and where
the stress falls, the page has already told you almost everything — with two exceptions. Spelling hides
**rhythm** and it hides **linking**. Between them, they are the reason a Spanish speaker talking at a
completely ordinary speed sounds to you as though they are talking fast.

## 1 · Every syllable is the same size

English is a stress-timed language. You hammer the strong syllables and crush everything between them:
*photography* comes out as **f'-TOG-r'-fee** — three vowels flattened into a neutral *uh*. French does
something different but equally un-Spanish: it gathers words into groups and puts one accent at the end of
each group, so the last syllable before a pause is the one that stretches.

Spanish does neither. **Every syllable gets roughly the same amount of time, and no vowel is ever reduced.**
The final *a* of *ensalada* is exactly the same *a* as the stressed one: **en-sa-LA-da**, four even beats,
four identical *a*s. One syllable is a little stronger than the others — but it is not strong at its
neighbours' expense, and the weak ones do not shrink.

This single habit is worth more than every consonant in this book put together.

An English speaker who says *en-s'-LAH-d'* has produced two vowels Spanish does not own, inside a word that
was otherwise perfect. A French speaker who says *en-sa-la-DA* has moved the beat to the end of the word,
which in Spanish is how you turn *hablo* (I speak) into *habló* (he spoke). Neither error is exotic. Both
are simply your first language running on autopilot.

> **Do this.** Tap the table once per syllable, evenly, like a metronome, and fit the word to your taps. If
> a tap lands on nothing, you swallowed a vowel. If two taps come out different lengths, slow down.

## 2 · Words do not have edges

Spanish is written with spaces. It is not spoken with them. Inside a breath group, a word ending in a
consonant hands that consonant to the next word when the next word begins with a vowel:

- *los amigos* → **lo-sa-mi-gos**
- *el hombre* → **e-lom-bre** (the *h* was never there)
- *un año* → **u-na-ño** — which is exactly why it can sound like *una*

And when two vowels meet across a space, they are squeezed into a single beat:

- *la escuela* → **laes-cue-la** — three beats, not four
- *mi amigo* → **mia-mi-go**
- *va a hablar* → **va-blar** — three *a*s become one

Nothing is being dropped or slurred. This is simply what a Spanish sentence *is*: a chain of syllables with
the word boundaries painted over. It also means that *tienes alas* and *tiene salas* are genuinely identical
in speech, and only context separates them.

> **Do this.** Take any line of dialogue. With a pencil, draw a small arc under every place where a final
> consonant meets a following vowel, and every place where two vowels touch across a space. Then read the
> line as one long word.

## 3 · The protocol — run this on every dialogue

Five passes. Ten minutes. Pencil in hand.

1. **Mark the beats.** Over every word of two or more syllables, put a dot on the stressed syllable. Written
   accent → the dot goes there. No accent and the word ends in a vowel, *-n* or *-s* → second-to-last
   syllable. No accent, any other ending → last syllable.
2. **Draw the chains.** Arcs for the linking, as above.
3. **Beat it out silently.** Tap once per syllable, evenly, right through the line, saying nothing. You are
   checking that you know how many beats there are before you commit your mouth to them.
4. **Say it — three times.** Once at half speed, with full short identical vowels, no puff of air on *p*,
   *t* or *c*, and every *r* a single flick unless it is *rr* or starts the word. Then once at normal speed.
   Then once slightly too fast.
5. **Record and check.** Phone, voice memo, one take. Play it back and ask three questions, and only these
   three: *Did the beat land on my pencil dots? Did any vowel turn into English "uh" or French "eu"? Did I
   stop between words?* Fix one thing, re-record once, and stop.

Do not try to judge your accent from the recording. You can't — you have nothing to compare it to. What you
*can* judge, alone and reliably, are those three things, because all three are decisions you made on paper
before you opened your mouth. That is the whole trick of working without audio: **only ever check yourself
against something you can see.**

## 4 · What this book cannot do

Everything above trains your mouth. None of it trains your ear, and the two are not the same skill.

By working through the **Suena así** boxes and the protocol above you can learn to produce Spanish with the
right vowels, the right stress, the right even rhythm and the right joins between words — and that is
genuinely most of what makes a speaker intelligible.

What you will not be able to do is *hear* Spanish. You will not recognise in someone else's speech the
linking you have learned to produce yourself; fast speech will still arrive as an undifferentiated stream.
You will have no reliable sense of whether your own *r*, your *j* or your five vowels actually match a
Spanish speaker's, because you have never heard one to compare against, and your own voice sounds different
from the inside. You will not acquire the rolled **rr** from a page — even advanced, taught learners mostly
don't. And you will get no correction: nothing in a book can tell you that the thing you have been
confidently repeating for six months is wrong.

Two things, eventually, are not optional: **a lot of listening**, so that your ear builds the categories
your mouth has been guessing at; and **at least a few hours with a person who will interrupt you** — a
teacher, a tutor, an exchange partner — because feedback is the single strongest ingredient in the research
on pronunciation teaching, and it is the one ingredient print cannot contain.

Treat this chapter as the best possible preparation for that day, not as a substitute for it.

> **About the respellings.** The **Suena así** boxes spell words out in English-friendly syllables
> (*la pizarra* → pee-THAH-rrah) with the strong beat in capitals. They are a scaffold, not the goal. Spanish
> spelling already tells you the sounds almost perfectly, and a made-up English-looking string will
> eventually teach you English habits. **By the middle of A2 you should be reading the Spanish and using the
> respelling only to check yourself — and then not needing it at all.**
>
> One more note: these boxes use the Spanish of Spain, where **z** and **c** before *e/i* are the *th* of
> *think*. In Latin America the same letters are a plain **s**. Both are completely standard.
