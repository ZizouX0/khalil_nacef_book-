# Culture boxes

## El primer día {#u=A1-0}
**Greetings follow the meal clock, not the number clock.** *Buenos días* lasts until the midday meal — which in Spain is around 2 p.m., not 12 — then *buenas tardes* takes over until it gets dark, and *buenas noches* serves as both "good evening" and "goodnight". Getting this wrong is the commonest first-day slip.

**Say hello to the room.** Walking into a class, a shop or a waiting room, a general *¡Hola, buenas!* is expected; saying nothing reads as cold. Between classmates, Spaniards greet with two kisses and most Latin Americans with one — but on a first day a handshake or a wave is never wrong.

**How to address the teacher.** In Spain, adult students normally use *tú* and the teacher's first name. In much of Latin America *usted* and *profe* or *profesor/profesora* are the norm. Start with *usted* and you will be told to switch.

**Keep the class in Spanish** with four phrases: ***¿Cómo se dice…?***, ***No entiendo***, ***¿Puedes repetir, por favor?*** and ***Más despacio, por favor***. And learn the local "OK": *vale* in Spain, *dale* in Argentina, *listo* in Colombia.

## Dos apellidos {#u=A1-1}
**Everyone has two surnames**, one from each parent. *Ana García Pérez* is *Ana*, and she is filed under ***García***, her first surname — in a register, an index or an alphabetical list, the second surname is only a tie-breaker. They are not middle names and they are not hyphenated. Since June 2017, Spanish parents choose which surname goes first; before that the father's came first automatically. And nobody changes their surname on marrying, anywhere in the Spanish-speaking world.

Forms therefore ask for three things — *nombre*, *primer apellido*, *segundo apellido*. If you have only one surname, write it in the first box and leave the second empty.

**Say *estadounidense*, not *americano*.** For most Latin Americans *América* is a single continent from Alaska to Patagonia, and they are Americans too; *soy estadounidense* or *soy de Estados Unidos* avoids the argument entirely.

Two more habits worth copying from this unit's dialogues: nationalities and languages are written in lower case (*soy alemana, hablo francés*), and the normal way to ask what someone does for a living is ***¿A qué te dedicas?***

## Lenguas del mundo hispano {#u=A1-2}
**Spanish is official in 21 countries, and almost nowhere is it the only language.** Spain has four co-official languages beside *castellano*: **Catalan/Valencian** (spoken by roughly 11% and 5% of Spaniards), **Galician** (about 5%), **Basque** — *el euskera*, unrelated to Spanish or to any other known language — (about 2%), and **Aranese**, with some 10,000 speakers in one Pyrenean valley. In Barcelona, Valencia, Bilbao or Santiago de Compostela, signs and menus may put the local language first: *sortida* and *saída* both mean *salida*. Saying ***Perdona, no hablo catalán, ¿me lo dices en castellano?*** is polite and completely normal.

**Latin America is just as multilingual.** Paraguay is genuinely bilingual — 38.7% of households use Guaraní and Spanish at home, and in the countryside over half use mainly Guaraní. Quechua, Aymara, Nahuatl and the Maya languages have millions of speakers between them.

**Free practice is an institution.** *Un intercambio* — an hour of your language, an hour of Spanish, usually in a café — is how thousands of people learn. Ask: ***¿Quedamos para un intercambio?***

## El verano al revés {#u=A1-3}
**South of the equator the seasons flip.** July is deep winter in Santiago de Chile, with ski resorts an hour from the city, while January and February are high summer — the months when Chileans and Argentines take their holidays and the cities empty. Christmas dinner in Santiago, Buenos Aires or Montevideo is eaten in the heat, often outdoors. Chile's national holiday, the *Fiestas Patrias* of **18 September**, is a spring festival. So when a Chilean says *el verano pasado* in March, they mean the January just gone.

**Altitude beats latitude.** Quito and Bogotá sit almost on the equator, but at 2,850 and 2,640 metres they hover around 14–20 °C all year. Pack a jacket, not shorts.

**Spain is not one climate either.** As Elena and Pablo say in this unit, Galicia in the northwest is green and rains all year, while Andalusia passes 40 °C in summer and the Tabernas desert in Almería is dry enough that spaghetti westerns were filmed there. Temperatures everywhere are in Celsius. Ask ***¿Qué tiempo hace?*** for today, and ***¿Qué tiempo hace en invierno?*** before you pack.

## Tallas y rebajas {#u=A1-4}
**The price on the label is the price you pay.** Shops quote *IVA incluido* — sales tax is already inside the number, so nothing is added at the till.

**Sizes do not travel.** Spain uses European numbers: women's clothes 34–46, shoes roughly 36–46. Mexico and much of Latin America label clothes **CH, M, G, EG** (*chico, mediano, grande, extra grande*), roughly S, M, L, XL, and number shoes their own way. So ask: ***¿Tiene la talla 40?*** and ***¿Me lo puedo probar?*** (the fitting room is *el probador*).

**Sales have a season.** *Las rebajas* traditionally begin around **7 January**, after Reyes, and again in early July. Since 2012 each shop may set its own dates, so discounts now turn up all year.

**Haggling is out of place** in a shop or a chain store; it belongs to a *mercadillo* or a craft market, and even there it is gentle.

Two habits to copy: greet with *Hola, buenas* when you walk in and *Gracias, adiós* when you leave, even if you buy nothing — and if you are only browsing, say ***Solo estoy mirando, gracias***.

## Familia y santo {#u=A1-5}
**Young adults live at home much longer.** Spaniards leave the family home at **30.2 on average** against a EU average of 26.3, and the pattern across Latin America is similar. The cause is rent, not immaturity: a 28-year-old living with their parents is unremarkable, and asking "why?" is not a compliment.

**Sunday lunch is the family fixture** — often three generations at one table, with grandparents doing a real share of the childcare during the week.

**Many people celebrate a second birthday.** Anyone named after a saint may be congratulated on *el día de su santo*: *Josés* on 19 March, *Pilares* on 12 October. ***¡Felicidades!*** works for both occasions; *¡Feliz cumpleaños!* only for the birthday.

**Affection shrinks words** — *abuelita*, *Juanito*, *un cafecito* — and, in much of Latin America, it produces nicknames a foreigner would misread: *gordo/a*, *flaco/a*, *negrito/a* are used warmly inside families. Understand them; don't imitate them.

In Mexico and much of Latin America a girl's fifteenth birthday, *los quince* or *la quinceañera*, is a bigger celebration than any birthday that follows.

## El horario español {#u=A1-6}
**The day runs about two hours later than you expect.** *El desayuno* (7–9) is small: coffee and toast. *La comida*, **14:00–15:30**, is the main meal. Children have *la merienda* around 17:30. *La cena* is **21:00–22:30**, and most restaurant kitchens do not open for dinner until 20:30 or 21:00 — turn up at 19:00 and you will find the chairs on the tables.

**Part of the reason is the clock itself.** Spain sits at roughly the longitude of Britain but keeps Central European Time, so on 21 June the sun sets in Madrid at **21:48**. Dinner at ten feels like dinner at eight.

**Latin America differs.** Mexico's *comida* is also mid-afternoon, but *la cena* is lighter and earlier, 19:00–21:00; Argentina and Uruguay eat as late as Spain, or later.

**Timetables are written in the 24-hour clock** (*20:30* on the door) but spoken as *las ocho y media de la tarde*.

**Punctuality depends on context.** Be on time for work, class, the doctor and the train. For dinner at a friend's house, arriving 15–30 minutes after the stated time is normal and expected.

## A la mesa {#u=A1-7}
**Lunch is the meal that matters**, and on weekdays the cheapest way to eat it is *el menú del día*: starter, main, bread, a drink and dessert or coffee for one price. The Spanish average was **€14.20** in 2025 — about €13 in the Canaries, €16 in the Balearics.

**Water is free if you ask for it.** Since 2022, Spanish bars and restaurants must offer non-bottled water at no charge: ***¿Me pone un vaso de agua del grifo, por favor?***

**The bill never comes until you ask.** Being left alone is good service, not neglect. Say ***La cuenta, por favor*** or ***¿Me cobra, por favor?***

**Tipping is not the same everywhere.** Spain: round up, or leave €1–2 a head, 5–10% for a memorable meal. Mexico: **10–15%** is expected. Chile: the bill arrives with a **10% *propina sugerida*** and most people simply pay it.

**Order with this unit's own verbs**: *¿Me pone una caña?* for what is served on the spot, *¿Me trae la carta?* for what is carried to the table. Ask for *un café solo* (espresso) or *un cortado* — but in Colombia ask for ***un tinto*** and you will get black coffee, not wine.

## Direcciones y colas {#u=A1-8}
**Addresses run from big to small and include the floor**: *Calle Mayor 12, 3.º izq.* — street, number, third floor, left-hand door. **Planta baja** is the ground floor, so *1.º* is one flight up, and what a North American calls the third floor is *2.º*. At the street door (*el portal*) you press the flat's button on the *portero automático* and say who you are. Distances are given in minutes on foot: *está a cinco minutos andando*.

**Queues often have no line.** In a pharmacy, a bakery, a market stall or a health centre, people stand or sit anywhere and simply remember the order. On arriving you ask ***¿Quién es el último?*** — or, commonly in Madrid, ***¿Quién da la vez?*** Whoever answers *yo* is the person you follow. Go in front of them and you will hear about it.

**The *barrio* is a unit of life**: its bar, its *farmacia* with a green cross, its *estanco* for stamps and transport cards. In smaller towns independent shops close roughly **14:00–17:00**, and Sunday opening is limited and set region by region, while malls and big chains stay open all day.

## Buscar trabajo {#u=A1-9}
**The Spanish *currículum* usually carries a photo.** It is not a legal requirement and attitudes are slowly changing, but a headshot — plus date of birth and often *carné de conducir: sí* — is still standard in Spain and in much of Latin America, and a CV without one can look unfinished.

**Salaries are quoted gross, per year, in fourteen payments.** Spanish law gives employees two extra payslips a year (*las pagas extras*, normally summer and Christmas) unless the collective agreement spreads them over twelve months. So *24.000 € brutos en 14 pagas* is about €1,714 gross a month, not €2,000 — always ask ***¿Son catorce pagas o doce?***

**Register in an interview.** Start with *usted*, shake hands — no kisses at a professional first meeting — and let the interviewer offer *puedes tutearme*. Expect exactly this unit's questions, and answer with *saber* + infinitive: ***Sé conducir***, ***Sé cocinar***, ***Sé hablar tres idiomas***.

**Paperwork first.** Non-EU citizens need an **NIE** and a work authorisation before signing a Spanish contract; elsewhere the equivalent number comes first too — *RFC* in Mexico, *CUIL* in Argentina, *RUT* in Chile.

## ¿Español o castellano? {#u=A2-1}
**Both names are correct.** *Español* is the usual international label; in Spain — where it stands beside Catalan, Galician and Basque — and across much of South America people say *castellano*. The Spanish Constitution itself uses *castellano*. Nobody is wrong, so use whichever your teacher uses and don't correct anyone.

**The language has twenty-three academies, not one.** The RAE in Madrid writes the dictionary, the grammar and the spelling rules jointly with the academies of the other Spanish-speaking countries (plus the Philippines, Equatorial Guinea and the United States). Norms are agreed across the whole language, so a Mexican or Argentine usage in the dictionary is as official as a Madrid one.

**Which "you all" to learn.** If you are studying for Spain, use *vosotros*. Everywhere in Latin America *ustedes* covers formal and informal plural alike, so you can simply skip the *vosotros* row and never be wrong.

**Certificates that count.** The *Instituto Cervantes* runs the **DELE** (fixed exam dates) and, with partner universities, **SIELE** (on screen, bookable most weeks). Both are graded A1–C2, the same scale as this book.

## Cine en español {#u=A2-2}
**Spain dubs; Latin America subtitles.** Around **90%** of foreign films in Spanish cinemas are shown dubbed, and dubbing there is a respected craft with its own stars. If you want the original soundtrack, look for **V.O.S.E.** in the listings — *versión original subtitulada en español*; the big city chains now programme it daily. In most of Latin America, adult films are subtitled and only children's films dubbed, so the same Hollywood title is *doblada* in Madrid and *subtitulada* in Mexico City.

**On a streaming app, choose your Spanish.** The audio and subtitle menus separate *español (España)* from *español (Latinoamérica)*. Pick the one you are learning, and put the subtitles in Spanish rather than English — exactly the advice Diego gives Lena in the previous unit.

**Dates in a biography are day-first**: *12/5/1996* is 12 May 1996, said *el doce de mayo de mil novecientos noventa y seis*.

**The prizes to know** are Spain's **Goya** awards each winter and Mexico's **Ariel**. Amenábar — born in Santiago de Chile, raised in Madrid — is a reminder that this is one shared industry.

## Alquilar un piso {#u=A2-3}
**The words change with the country.** Spain: *el piso*, *alquilar*, *el alquiler*. Mexico and most of Latin America: *el departamento* or *el apartamento*; in Chile and Colombia you *arrendar* and pay *el arriendo*.

**Read the advert.** *Exterior* means the windows give onto the street; *interior* means they face the building's inner light well — cheaper, quieter, darker. *Sin amueblar* means empty, sometimes down to the light fittings. *Gastos no incluidos* means community charges, water and heating are on top, so ask first: ***¿Los gastos están incluidos?***

**The money.** For a main home the deposit (*la fianza*) is **one month's rent**, and since **26 May 2023** the estate agency's fee in Spain is legally the landlord's to pay, not the tenant's. If an agency asks you for a *mes de agencia*, it is asking for something the law now forbids.

**Floors matter**: *un cuarto sin ascensor* is cheap for an obvious reason.

**Sharing is normal well into your thirties** in Madrid, Barcelona, Mexico City or Buenos Aires. *Busco habitación en piso compartido* is an ordinary sentence at any age.

## Besos, tú y usted {#u=A2-4}
**Two kisses, one kiss, or a handshake.** Meeting socially in Spain, women greet women and men with *dos besos* — right cheek first, cheek against cheek, no sound effect and no lips. Men greet men with a handshake or a hug. In Argentina, Uruguay and Chile it is **one** kiss, and in Argentina men kiss men too. In Mexico and Colombia, one kiss between women or mixed pairs. At any professional first meeting, anywhere, a handshake is correct.

***Tú* or *usted*.** Spain leans heavily on *tú* — with waiters, shop staff, colleagues, teachers — keeping *usted* for older strangers, officials and doctors. Colombia and Costa Rica do the opposite: *usted* between friends, couples, even to children. Argentina and Uruguay use *vos*. With anyone older, start with *usted* and wait for ***Puedes tutearme*** or ***Nos tuteamos, ¿no?***

**Direct is not rude.** *¿Me pones un café?* — or even *Ponme un café* — is perfectly polite; Spanish carries warmth in the tone, not in a "please" per sentence. In Mexico you will hear ***¿Mande?*** where you expected *¿Qué?*

**Refusing something?** Soften it with *es que…*

## Horarios del ocio {#u=A2-5}
**Museums have free hours — use them.** The Prado is free **18:00–20:00 Monday to Saturday and 17:00–19:00 on Sundays and holidays**; the Reina Sofía and many municipal museums have their own free slots, and students and over-65s pay *entrada reducida* on production of ID. Big sights with timed tickets — the Alhambra, the Sagrada Família, Machu Picchu — sell out weeks ahead, so book online rather than at the door.

**The night starts late.** Dinner 21:00–22:30, bars until two or three, clubs filling around one and closing at six in the morning, as this unit's examples say. Nobody meets for a drink at six p.m.

**Tapas are free in three cities, not everywhere**: in **Granada, León and Jaén** a drink comes with a real plate of food. In Madrid or Barcelona you order them and pay.

**Watch for *el puente*.** Spain has up to 14 public holidays a year; when one falls on a Tuesday or Thursday the country takes the Monday or Friday too, and transport and hotels fill. August is the other rush, when much of Spain is away and small shops close for a fortnight.

Keep two questions ready: ***¿A qué hora abre?*** and ***¿Hay entrada reducida para estudiantes?***

## Mercado y sobremesa {#u=A2-6}
**Buy by weight.** At a market stall or a supermarket counter you ask for *medio kilo de tomates*, *un cuarto de jamón*, *cien gramos de queso*, *un litro de leche* — the measures this unit teaches. Take a numbered ticket or ask *¿Quién es el último?*, and expect *¿Algo más?* before *¿Le cobro?*

**Say what you cannot eat, clearly.** Vegetarian and vegan options are far easier to find in cities than a decade ago, but assumptions differ: *jamón*, or a meat stock, is not always counted as "meat" by an older cook. Say ***Soy vegetariano/a: ¿lleva carne, pescado o jamón?***, and for an allergy ***Soy alérgico/a a…*** — name it.

**Staples are regional, not "Hispanic".** Spain runs on olive oil and bread; Mexico on *maíz* and tortillas at every meal; Peru on potatoes, with thousands of native varieties; Argentina on the Sunday *asado*.

**Then there is *la sobremesa*** — the stretch after the plates are empty when everyone stays at the table talking, over coffee, often for an hour. Standing up as soon as you have eaten looks abrupt. Nobody will rush you, and nobody will bring the bill.

## ¿Qué tal el viaje? {#u=A2-7}
**One question, two tenses.** In Spain you will be asked *¿Has estado alguna vez en México?*; across most of Latin America the same question comes out as *¿Fuiste alguna vez a México?* The split is regional, not a mistake, and either version is understood everywhere.

**Answer with feeling, not facts.** *¿Qué tal?* expects ***Me lo pasé genial***, ***Me encantó***, ***La gente me cayó muy bien*** — the structures this unit drills. A flat "it was fine" sounds as if you hated it.

**Distances are bigger than the map suggests.** Chile is over 4,000 km long. Overnight coaches are a normal, comfortable way to cross Argentina or Chile, with seats sold as *semi-cama* (reclining) or *cama* (almost flat) — pay for *cama* on anything over ten hours.

**Altitude is real.** Quito sits at 2,850 m, Cusco at 3,400 m, La Paz at 3,600 m, and *el soroche* (altitude sickness) hits fit people too: arrive, do nothing on the first day, eat lightly, accept the *mate de coca* if it is offered.

And the phrase that opens every door: ***¿Qué me recomiendas ver?***

## Si te pones malo {#u=A2-8}
**The pharmacy is the first stop, not the doctor.** Spanish *farmacéuticos* are trained to advise on minor problems and can sell *paracetamol* 650 mg or *ibuprofeno* 400 mg over the counter. **Antibiotics always need a prescription** (*una receta*) in Spain — no exceptions. Every pharmacy shows a green cross, and every closed one posts the nearest **farmacia de guardia**, the round-the-clock rota, on its door and online.

**For something serious.** You *pedir cita* at your *centro de salud*, or go straight to *urgencias*. EU visitors use the European Health Insurance Card (*la Tarjeta Sanitaria Europea*); everyone else needs travel insurance. The emergency number is **112** in Spain and across the EU, **911** in Mexico.

**Latin America is looser at the counter**: many pharmacies there sell medicines Spain restricts, sometimes including antibiotics. That is a convenience, not an invitation — self-prescribing antibiotics is a bad idea anywhere.

**Phrases that get you helped**: ***No me encuentro bien***, ***Me duele la garganta***, ***Tengo fiebre***, ***¿Tiene algo para la tos?*** And when you say you are ill, people answer ***que te mejores*** — get well soon.

## En una generación {#u=A2-9}
**Everything in this unit happened within living memory.** Spain was a dictatorship from 1939 until Franco died on **20 November 1975**; the *Transición* followed, the current Constitution was approved by referendum in **1978**, and Spain joined what is now the EU in **1986**. In South America the dates overlap: Chile's dictatorship ran **1973–1990**, ended by the *plebiscito* of 1988 in which *el No* won; Argentina's ran **1976–1983**.

So when the grandmother in this unit's dialogue says they had no television and she took the train to the village once a year, she is describing a country that was poor, rural and sending its people abroad — the same country that now receives them.

**Events carry shorthand names** that people use without explaining: *el 23-F* (the attempted coup of 1981), *el 11-M* (the Madrid bombings of 2004), *el 18* (Chile's national day).

**One word of care.** Asking an older person ***¿Cómo era la vida antes?*** is usually welcomed and will earn you an hour of beautiful *imperfecto*. The Civil War and the dictatorship, though, still divide families — that is a subject to listen to, not to pronounce on.

## Contar una anécdota {#u=A2-10}
**A story is a joint performance.** Spanish conversation overlaps: interrupting, finishing someone's sentence and talking at the same time signal interest, not rudeness. The listener is expected to keep reacting — ***¿En serio?***, ***¡No me digas!***, ***¿Y qué pasó?***, ***¡Qué fuerte!*** — and a silent listener reads as a bored one. Learn three of these and you can hold up your half of a conversation long before your grammar is ready.

**Open the way Sara does**: *¿Sabes qué me pasó el otro día?* Set the scene in the imperfecto (*estaba paseando, era de noche, no llevaba las gafas*), let the events land in the indefinido, and keep the twist for the end: *Resulta que era mi vecino.*

**Embarrassment has its own vocabulary**: *¡Qué corte!*, *¡Qué vergüenza!*, *pasar vergüenza*, *pasar miedo*.

**Some anecdotes belong to a whole country.** Earthquakes are part of life in Chile and Mexico: Mexico holds a national drill every **19 September at noon**, marking the quakes of 1985 and 2017, with the alarm sounding on every phone. Chileans distinguish *un temblor* — routine, keep drinking your coffee — from *un terremoto*.
