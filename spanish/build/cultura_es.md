# Culture boxes

## El primer día {#u=A1-0}
The first thing that goes wrong is the greeting, and it goes wrong because Spanish measures the day by meals rather than by numbers. *Buenos días* runs until the midday meal, which in Spain happens at around 2 p.m., not 12. After that it is *buenas tardes* until the light goes, and then *buenas noches*, which does the work of both "good evening" and "goodnight" — you can use it to arrive and to leave.

Walk into a class, a shop or a waiting room and greet the room: *¡Hola, buenas!* Say nothing and you come across as cold, which is not what you meant. Between classmates, Spaniards kiss twice and most Latin Americans once. On a first day, nobody minds a handshake or a wave.

Your teacher is a separate question. In Spain, adult students use *tú* and the teacher's first name. In much of Latin America it is *usted*, and *profe* or *profesor/profesora*. Start with *usted*; somebody will tell you to stop.

Four phrases keep the whole class in Spanish: ***¿Cómo se dice…?***, ***No entiendo***, ***¿Puedes repetir, por favor?*** and ***Más despacio, por favor***. And learn the local yes-fine-alright: *vale* in Spain, *dale* in Argentina, *listo* in Colombia.

## Dos apellidos {#u=A1-1}
*Ana García Pérez* is *Ana*. Not Ana Pérez, and there is no middle name hiding in there. She has two surnames, one from each parent, and she is filed under the first: look her up under ***García***, with *Pérez* only as a tie-breaker when the register throws up a second Ana García. They are never hyphenated. Since June 2017 Spanish parents choose which surname goes first; before that the father's went first automatically. And nobody changes their surname on marrying, anywhere in the Spanish-speaking world.

Which is why forms ask for three things: *nombre*, *primer apellido*, *segundo apellido*. One surname only? Put it in the first box and leave the second empty.

Say *estadounidense*, not *americano*. For most Latin Americans *América* is a single continent running from Alaska to Patagonia, and they are Americans too; *soy estadounidense* or *soy de Estados Unidos* keeps you out of that conversation for good.

Two more habits from this unit's dialogues: nationalities and languages take no capital letter (*soy alemana, hablo francés*), and you ask what somebody does for a living with ***¿A qué te dedicas?***

## Lenguas del mundo hispano {#u=A1-2}
The word on the sign is *sortida*, or it is *saída*. Both mean *salida*, and if you are standing in a station in Barcelona or Santiago de Compostela wondering where the exit has gone, that is your answer. The sign is not in Spanish.

Spanish is official in 21 countries and almost nowhere is it the only language. Spain has four more beside *castellano*. **Catalan/Valencian** is spoken by roughly 11% and 5% of Spaniards, **Galician** by about 5%, **Basque** by about 2% — *el euskera*, which is not related to Spanish, or to French, or to any other language anybody has ever managed to find. **Aranese** has some 10,000 speakers in one Pyrenean valley and is co-official too. In Barcelona, Valencia, Bilbao and Santiago, signs and menus may put the local language first. Saying ***Perdona, no hablo catalán, ¿me lo dices en castellano?*** is polite and completely ordinary.

Latin America is the same story on a larger scale. Paraguay is properly bilingual: 38.7% of households use Guaraní and Spanish at home, and in the countryside more than half use mainly Guaraní. Quechua, Aymara, Nahuatl and the Maya languages have millions of speakers between them.

One institution worth knowing about: *un intercambio*, an hour of your language and an hour of Spanish, usually in a café. It is how thousands of people learn, and it is free. Ask: ***¿Quedamos para un intercambio?***

## El verano al revés {#u=A1-3}
A Chilean who says *el verano pasado* in March means the January that has just gone.

South of the equator the year is upside down. July is deep winter in Santiago de Chile, with ski resorts an hour from the city, and January and February are the high summer when Chileans and Argentines vanish on holiday and the cities empty out. Christmas dinner in Santiago, Buenos Aires and Montevideo is eaten in the heat, very often outdoors. Chile's national holiday, the *Fiestas Patrias* of **18 September**, is a spring festival.

Height beats latitude. Quito and Bogotá sit almost on the equator, but at 2,850 and 2,640 metres they hover around 14–20 °C all year, every month, forever. Pack a jacket, not shorts.

Spain is not one climate either. Elena and Pablo say as much in this unit: Galicia in the northwest is green and rains all year, while Andalusia goes past 40 °C in summer and the Tabernas desert in Almería is dry enough that they filmed spaghetti westerns in it.

Everything is in Celsius. ***¿Qué tiempo hace?*** asks about today. ***¿Qué tiempo hace en invierno?*** is the one to ask before you pack.

## Tallas y rebajas {#u=A1-4}
It took me an embarrassing number of shops to stop bracing for a surprise at the till. There isn't one. Prices are quoted *IVA incluido*: the tax is already inside the number on the label.

Sizes do not travel. Spain uses European numbers, women's clothes 34–46 and shoes roughly 36–46; Mexico and much of Latin America use **CH, M, G, EG** (*chico, mediano, grande, extra grande*), roughly S, M, L, XL, and number shoes their own way. So ask: ***¿Tiene la talla 40?*** and ***¿Me lo puedo probar?*** The fitting room is *el probador*.

*Las rebajas* traditionally start around **7 January**, once Reyes is over, and again in early July. Since 2012 each shop sets its own dates, so discounts now turn up all year.

Do not haggle in a shop or a chain. It belongs to a *mercadillo* or a craft market, and even there it is gentle.

Say *Hola, buenas* walking in and *Gracias, adiós* walking out, bought or not. If you are only looking: ***Solo estoy mirando, gracias***.

## Familia y santo {#u=A1-5}
Spaniards leave the family home at **30.2 on average**, against an EU average of 26.3, and Latin America runs much the same. The reason is rent, not arrested development. A 28-year-old still living with their parents is unremarkable, and asking why is not the light question it sounds like.

Sunday lunch holds the week together: three generations at one table, and grandparents who do a real share of the childcare in between.

Plenty of people get two birthdays. Anyone named after a saint gets congratulated on *el día de su santo*: the *Josés* on 19 March, the *Pilares* on 12 October. ***¡Felicidades!*** covers both. *¡Feliz cumpleaños!* covers only the birthday.

Affection shrinks words: *abuelita*, *Juanito*, *un cafecito*. Across much of Latin America it also produces nicknames a foreigner reads as insults — *gordo/a*, *flaco/a*, *negrito/a* — used warmly inside families, between people who have earned them. Understand them. Do not copy them.

And in Mexico and much of Latin America, a girl's fifteenth birthday, *los quince* or *la quinceañera*, outranks every birthday that follows it.

## El horario español {#u=A1-6}
Turn up for dinner at 19:00 and you will find the chairs on the tables. Most restaurant kitchens do not open for the evening until 20:30 or 21:00, and *la cena* runs **21:00–22:30**.

The whole day sits about two hours later than you expect it to. *El desayuno* (7–9) is coffee and toast and not much else. *La comida*, **14:00–15:30**, is the real meal. Children get *la merienda* around 17:30 to bridge the gap, and they need it.

Part of this is the clock itself. Spain lies at roughly the longitude of Britain but keeps Central European Time, so on 21 June the sun goes down in Madrid at **21:48**. Dinner at ten feels like dinner at eight. I have been going for years and I still cannot make myself hungry at that hour.

Latin America is not Spain. Mexico takes *la comida* mid-afternoon too, but *la cena* is lighter and earlier, 19:00–21:00. Argentina and Uruguay eat as late as Spain, sometimes later.

Timetables are written in the 24-hour clock (*20:30* on the door) and spoken as *las ocho y media de la tarde*.

Punctuality depends entirely on what you are late for. Work, class, the doctor, the train: be on time. Dinner at a friend's flat: turn up 15–30 minutes after the hour they gave you, because that is the hour they meant.

## A la mesa {#u=A1-7}
Lunch is the meal that matters, and on a weekday the cheapest way to eat it properly is *el menú del día*: a starter, a main, bread, a drink and either dessert or coffee, all for one price. The Spanish average was **€14.20** in 2025 — about €13 in the Canaries, €16 in the Balearics.

Water is free if you ask for it. Since 2022, Spanish bars and restaurants have been obliged to offer non-bottled water at no charge: ***¿Me pone un vaso de agua del grifo, por favor?***

The bill will not come until you ask for it, and this is the part that unsettles people. Being left alone at your table is good service, not neglect. Nobody is waiting for you to leave. Say ***La cuenta, por favor*** or ***¿Me cobra, por favor?***

Tipping is not one rule. Spain: round up, or leave €1–2 a head, 5–10% if the meal was memorable. Mexico: **10–15%**, and it is expected. Chile: the bill arrives with a **10%** *propina sugerida* already printed on it, and most people simply pay it.

Order with this unit's two verbs. *¿Me pone una caña?* for whatever is poured on the spot; *¿Me trae la carta?* for whatever is carried to your table. Ask for *un café solo* or *un cortado* — but in Colombia, ask for ***un tinto*** and what arrives is black coffee, not wine.

## Direcciones y colas {#u=A1-8}
There is no queue. There is an order, and everyone in the room is quietly keeping track of it.

In a pharmacy, a bakery, a market or a health centre, people stand and sit wherever they like. What you do on arriving is ask ***¿Quién es el último?***, or, commonly in Madrid, ***¿Quién da la vez?*** Whoever answers *yo* is the person you go after. Walk in front of them and you will hear about it.

Addresses run from big to small and include the floor. *Calle Mayor 12, 3.º izq.* is street, number, third floor, left-hand door. **Planta baja** is the ground floor, which means *1.º* is one flight up and what a North American calls the third floor is *2.º*. At the street door, *el portal*, you press the flat's button on the *portero automático* and say who you are. Distance is measured in minutes on foot: *está a cinco minutos andando*.

The *barrio* is a unit of life rather than a postcode — its bar, its *farmacia* under a green cross, its *estanco* for stamps and transport cards. In smaller towns the independent shops shut roughly **14:00–17:00**, and Sunday opening is limited and set region by region. The malls and the big chains stay open all day regardless.

## Buscar trabajo {#u=A1-9}
Put a photograph on it. The Spanish *currículum* normally carries a headshot, along with your date of birth and often a line reading *carné de conducir: sí*. None of it is legally required and attitudes are shifting, but a CV without a photo still looks half-finished to a great many recruiters in Spain and across Latin America.

Then the number that catches everyone out. Salaries are quoted gross, per year, and paid in fourteen instalments: Spanish law gives employees two extra payslips a year, *las pagas extras*, normally in summer and at Christmas, unless the collective agreement spreads them over twelve months instead. So *24.000 € brutos en 14 pagas* is about €1,714 gross a month, not €2,000. Ask outright: ***¿Son catorce pagas o doce?***

In the interview, start with *usted* and shake hands. No kisses at a professional first meeting. Wait for the interviewer to offer *puedes tutearme*. The questions will be exactly the ones in this unit, and the answers are *saber* plus an infinitive: ***Sé conducir***, ***Sé cocinar***, ***Sé hablar tres idiomas***.

Paperwork comes before any of it. Non-EU citizens need an **NIE** and a work authorisation before signing a Spanish contract, and elsewhere the equivalent number comes first too: *RFC* in Mexico, *CUIL* in Argentina, *RUT* in Chile.

## ¿Español o castellano? {#u=A2-1}
Both names are right. *Español* is the usual international label; in Spain, where the language stands beside Catalan, Galician and Basque, and across much of South America, people say *castellano*. The Constitution itself says *castellano*. Use whichever word your teacher uses, and correct nobody.

Twenty-three academies, not one. The RAE in Madrid writes the dictionary, the grammar and the spelling rules jointly with the academies of the other Spanish-speaking countries, plus the Philippines, Equatorial Guinea and the United States. Norms are agreed across the whole language, so a Mexican or Argentine usage in the dictionary is exactly as official as a Madrid one. There is no head office.

Studying for Spain? Learn *vosotros*. If not, leave that row of the table blank for life: everywhere in Latin America, *ustedes* covers formal and informal plural alike.

Certificates come from the *Instituto Cervantes* — the **DELE**, on fixed exam dates, and **SIELE**, run with partner universities, on screen and bookable most weeks. Both graded A1–C2, the same scale as this book.

## Cine en español {#u=A2-2}
Around **90%** of foreign films in Spanish cinemas are shown dubbed, and dubbing in Spain is a respected craft with its own stars. For the original soundtrack, look for **V.O.S.E.** in the listings: *versión original subtitulada en español*. The big city chains programme it daily now.

Latin America does the opposite. Adult films are subtitled and only children's films dubbed, so the same Hollywood title is *doblada* in Madrid and *subtitulada* in Mexico City.

On a streaming app you choose. The audio and subtitle menus separate *español (España)* from *español (Latinoamérica)*. Pick the one you are learning, and set the subtitles to Spanish rather than English — the advice Diego gives Lena in the previous unit, and the most useful sentence in this box.

Dates in a biography run day first: *12/5/1996* is 12 May 1996, said *el doce de mayo de mil novecientos noventa y seis*.

The prizes to know are Spain's **Goya** awards each winter and Mexico's **Ariel**. Amenábar, born in Santiago de Chile and raised in Madrid, is a reminder that all of this is one shared industry.

## Alquilar un piso {#u=A2-3}
If an agency asks you for a *mes de agencia*, it is asking for money the law no longer lets it take: since **26 May 2023** the estate agency's fee in Spain belongs to the landlord, not the tenant. The deposit, *la fianza*, is **one month's rent** for a main home. That is the whole money conversation, and it is worth having it straight before you walk in.

Vocabulary shifts with the country. Spain: *el piso*, *alquilar*, *el alquiler*. Mexico and most of Latin America: *el departamento* or *el apartamento*. In Chile and Colombia you *arrendar* and pay *el arriendo*.

Then read the advert properly. *Exterior* means the windows give onto the street; *interior* means they open onto the building's inner light well, so it is cheaper, quieter, darker. *Sin amueblar* means empty, sometimes down to the light fittings. *Gastos no incluidos* means community charges, water and heating come on top, so ask: ***¿Los gastos están incluidos?***

Floors matter. *Un cuarto sin ascensor* is cheap, and you already know why.

Sharing is ordinary well into your thirties in Madrid, Barcelona, Mexico City and Buenos Aires. *Busco habitación en piso compartido* is a sentence people write at any age.

## Besos, tú y usted {#u=A2-4}
Right cheek first. Cheek against cheek, no lips, and no kissing noise — the noise is what gives a foreigner away. Meeting socially in Spain, women greet both women and men with *dos besos*, and men greet men with a handshake or a hug. In Argentina, Uruguay and Chile it is **one** kiss, and in Argentina men kiss men too. Mexico and Colombia: one kiss between women or in mixed pairs. At a professional first meeting, anywhere at all, a handshake is correct.

*Tú* or *usted* is harder, because the countries disagree with each other. Spain leans heavily on *tú* — with waiters, shop staff, colleagues, teachers — and keeps *usted* for older strangers, officials and doctors. Colombia and Costa Rica run the other way: *usted* between friends, between couples, even to children. Argentina and Uruguay use *vos*. With anyone older than you, start at *usted* and wait to be released by ***Puedes tutearme*** or ***Nos tuteamos, ¿no?***

Being direct is not being rude. *¿Me pones un café?*, or even *Ponme un café*, is perfectly polite. Spanish carries the warmth in the tone of voice rather than in a "please" bolted onto every sentence, and piling on English politeness formulas will make you sound odd rather than charming. In Mexico you will hear ***¿Mande?*** where you were expecting *¿Qué?*

Turning something down? Open with *es que…*

## Horarios del ocio {#u=A2-5}
The Prado is free **18:00–20:00 Monday to Saturday and 17:00–19:00 on Sundays and holidays**. The Reina Sofía has its own free slot, so do many municipal museums, and students and the over-65s pay *entrada reducida* on production of an ID card. Use all of it. What you cannot do is turn up at the Alhambra, the Sagrada Família or Machu Picchu and expect to get in — those sell timed tickets weeks ahead, so book online.

The evening starts later than you think. Dinner 21:00–22:30, bars until two or three, clubs filling around one and closing at six in the morning, which is what this unit's examples are describing. Nobody meets for a drink at six p.m.

Free tapas are real in exactly three cities. In **Granada, León and Jaén** your drink arrives with a proper plate of food. In Madrid or Barcelona you order tapas and you pay for them.

Watch for *el puente*. Spain has up to 14 public holidays a year, and when one lands on a Tuesday or a Thursday the country takes the Monday or the Friday as well; transport and hotels fill. August is the other stampede, when much of Spain is away and small shops close for a fortnight.

Keep two questions loaded: ***¿A qué hora abre?*** and ***¿Hay entrada reducida para estudiantes?***

## Mercado y sobremesa {#u=A2-6}
At a market stall or a supermarket counter you buy by weight: *medio kilo de tomates*, *un cuarto de jamón*, *cien gramos de queso*, *un litro de leche*, which are the measures this unit teaches. Take a numbered ticket, or ask *¿Quién es el último?* if there is no machine. You will get *¿Algo más?* before you get *¿Le cobro?*

Be blunt about what you cannot eat. Vegetarian and vegan food is far easier to find in cities than it was a decade ago, but the assumptions have not all caught up: to an older cook, *jamón* is not necessarily meat, and neither is a stock made from chicken. So say ***Soy vegetariano/a: ¿lleva carne, pescado o jamón?*** For an allergy, ***Soy alérgico/a a…*** and then name it. Do not be shy about this one.

There is no "Hispanic" diet. Spain runs on olive oil and bread, Mexico on *maíz* and tortillas at every meal, Peru on potatoes it grows in thousands of native varieties, Argentina on the Sunday *asado*.

And then there is *la sobremesa*: the stretch after the plates are empty when nobody moves, the coffee arrives and the talking goes on for an hour. Standing up the moment you have finished eating looks abrupt. Nobody will rush you, and nobody will bring the bill.

## ¿Qué tal el viaje? {#u=A2-7}
Answer with feeling, not with facts. *¿Qué tal?* is not asking for your itinerary; it is asking how it was. The expected replies are ***Me lo pasé genial***, ***Me encantó***, ***La gente me cayó muy bien*** — the structures this unit drills. A flat "it was fine" lands as though you hated the place and are being polite about it.

The same question also arrives in two tenses. In Spain: *¿Has estado alguna vez en México?* Across most of Latin America: *¿Fuiste alguna vez a México?* The split is regional rather than a mistake, and both versions are understood everywhere.

Distances are worse than the map suggests. Chile is over 4,000 km long. The overnight coach is a normal and comfortable way to cross Argentina or Chile, with seats sold as *semi-cama*, which reclines, or *cama*, which is almost flat. On anything over ten hours, pay for *cama*.

Altitude is not a rumour. Quito sits at 2,850 m, Cusco at 3,400 m, La Paz at 3,600 m, and *el soroche* — altitude sickness — flattens fit people as happily as anyone else. Arrive, do nothing on the first day, eat lightly, and accept the *mate de coca* if it is offered.

Then the question that opens every door: ***¿Qué me recomiendas ver?***

## Si te pones malo {#u=A2-8}
Go to the pharmacy first. Spanish *farmacéuticos* are trained to advise on minor problems and will sell you *paracetamol* 650 mg or *ibuprofeno* 400 mg over the counter. **Antibiotics always need a prescription** (*una receta*) in Spain — no exceptions, and no persuading anybody. Every pharmacy shows a green cross, and every closed one posts the nearest **farmacia de guardia** on its door and online: the rota that keeps one open round the clock.

For anything more serious, *pedir cita* at your *centro de salud*, or go straight to *urgencias*. EU visitors use the European Health Insurance Card (*la Tarjeta Sanitaria Europea*); everybody else needs travel insurance, bought before they travel. The emergency number is **112** in Spain and across the EU, **911** in Mexico.

Latin America is looser at the counter. Many pharmacies there sell medicines Spain restricts, antibiotics sometimes included. That is a convenience, not an invitation; prescribing yourself antibiotics is a bad idea in any country.

The phrases that get you looked after: ***No me encuentro bien***, ***Me duele la garganta***, ***Tengo fiebre***, ***¿Tiene algo para la tos?*** And when you tell somebody you are ill, the answer comes back ***que te mejores***. Get well soon.

## En una generación {#u=A2-9}
None of this is history in the safe, settled sense. It happened to people who are still alive.

Spain was a dictatorship from 1939 until Franco died on **20 November 1975**. Then came the *Transición*, the current Constitution approved by referendum in **1978**, and membership of what is now the EU in **1986**. In South America the dates overlap: Chile's dictatorship ran **1973–1990** and was ended by the *plebiscito* of 1988, the one in which *el No* won; Argentina's ran **1976–1983**.

So when the grandmother in this unit's dialogue says they had no television and that she took the train to the village once a year, she is describing a country that was poor, rural and sending its people abroad. It is the same country that now receives them.

Certain events carry shorthand names that people use without explaining: *el 23-F* is the attempted coup of 1981, *el 11-M* the Madrid bombings of 2004, *el 18* Chile's national day.

One word of care. Ask an older person ***¿Cómo era la vida antes?*** and you will usually be welcomed, and rewarded with an hour of beautiful *imperfecto*. The Civil War and the dictatorship are a different matter. They still divide families, and they are a subject to listen to rather than to have opinions about.

## Contar una anécdota {#u=A2-10}
A silent listener is a bored listener. That is the rule that catches out anybody raised to wait politely for their turn: in Spanish, interrupting, finishing the other person's sentence and talking over the top of them all signal interest. The listener's job is to keep making noise — ***¿En serio?***, ***¡No me digas!***, ***¿Y qué pasó?***, ***¡Qué fuerte!*** Learn three of those and you can hold up your half of a conversation long before your grammar is ready for it. It is the cheapest trick in the book, and it works.

Open the way Sara does: *¿Sabes qué me pasó el otro día?* Build the set in the imperfecto (*estaba paseando, era de noche, no llevaba las gafas*), let the events land in the indefinido, and save the turn for the last line: *Resulta que era mi vecino.*

Embarrassment has its own vocabulary, which tells you something about the priorities: *¡Qué corte!*, *¡Qué vergüenza!*, *pasar vergüenza*, *pasar miedo*.

Some anecdotes belong to a whole country. Earthquakes are ordinary life in Chile and Mexico. Mexico runs a national drill every **19 September at noon**, marking the quakes of 1985 and 2017, and the alarm sounds on every phone in the country. Chileans separate *un temblor*, which is routine and does not interrupt your coffee, from *un terremoto*, which does.
