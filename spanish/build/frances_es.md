# French help boxes

## Usted et le vouvoiement {#u=A1-1}
*Usted* ≍ *vous* de politesse — same job, two differences worth knowing on day one.

**Grammar.** *Vous* is a second person (*vous êtes*); *usted* is grammatically a **third** person and takes the same verb form as *él/ella*: *usted es*, *usted tiene* — never "usted eres". And Spain keeps a separate informal plural, *vosotros/as*, where French folds everything into *vous*; Latin America uses *ustedes* for every plural "you", polite or not.

**Socially.** Spanish moves to *tú* far faster than French moves to *tu*. In Spain a shop assistant, a colleague or a teacher of adults will be *tú* almost at once, and *usted* is reserved for the elderly, officials and formal service. In Colombia, Costa Rica and the Andes, *usted* lasts much longer — even inside the family. Listen to the form the other person uses, and copy it.

## Ser, estar : aucun secours {#u=A1-3}
Be warned: this is the one page of Spanish grammar where French gives you **nothing**. *Être* covers both verbs, so you cannot translate your way out — you are in exactly the same position as an English speaker, and your instinct will be wrong as often as theirs.

*Je suis professeur* → *Soy profesor*. *Je suis fatigué* → *Estoy cansado*. *Elle est jolie* (a trait) → *Es guapa*; *elle est jolie ce soir* (tonight) → *Está guapa*. Learn *ser* and *estar* as two separate verbs with separate jobs, never as one *être* cut in half.

One consolation in this unit: **hay ≍ il y a**, an exact match. Both stay invariable — *hay dos libros* ≍ *il y a deux livres* — where English must choose between "there is" and "there are".

## Setenta, ochenta, noventa {#u=A1-4}
Pure relief. Spanish counts in tens all the way up, with no arithmetic: **setenta** (70), **ochenta** (80), **noventa** (90) — no *soixante-dix*, no *quatre-vingts*, no *quatre-vingt-dix*. So 71 is *setenta y uno*, 92 is *noventa y dos*, 99 is *noventa y nueve*. (If you learned your numbers in Belgium or Switzerland, *septante* and *nonante* already prepared you for this.)

Two things French does **not** do, though. Hundreds agree in gender: *doscientos euros*, but *doscientas personas*. And *cien* shortens before a noun while *ciento* appears in compounds: *cien euros*, *ciento veinte*. Otherwise *mil euros* ≍ *mille euros*, with no article — it is English that insists on "a thousand".

## Ça me plaît {#u=A1-5}
English "I like" hides what is going on; French shows it. *Me gusta el cine* is built exactly like *le cinéma me plaît*: the thing liked is the **subject**, and the person is an **indirect object**. So the verb follows the thing, not you: *me gustan las películas* ≍ *les films me plaisent*.

Everything else follows from that. The reinforcement *a mí me gusta* ≍ *moi, ça me plaît*. The article stays, exactly as in French: *me gusta el café* ≍ *j'aime le café*, where English drops it. And *me encanta* ≍ *ça me plaît beaucoup / j'adore*.

One difference of register: *me gusta* is completely ordinary in Spanish — it is simply *how* you say you like something, far more common than *ça me plaît* is in French.

## Je me lève, me levanto {#u=A1-6}
Same machinery, bolt for bolt: a pronoun that matches the subject and sits in front of the verb. *Me levanto, te levantas, se levanta* ≍ *je me lève, tu te lèves, il se lève*. With an infinitive, both languages move the pronoun onto it — Spanish simply glues it to the end: *antes de acostarme* ≍ *avant de me coucher*.

A bonus you already have: with parts of the body, both languages use the **article**, not a possessive — *me lavo las manos* ≍ *je me lave les mains*, where English says "my hands".

The one thing to un-learn now, before unit A1-9: compound tenses take **haber**, never **ser**. *Me he levantado* ≍ *je me suis levé* — and no agreement, ever ("soy levantado" and "me he levantada" are both impossible).

## Les pronoms avant le verbe {#u=A1-7}
This is one of the biggest wins French gives you. *Lo veo* ≍ *je le vois*. *Te llamo* ≍ *je t'appelle*. *Le escribo* ≍ *je lui écris*. English puts the object after the verb ("I see it"); French and Spanish both make it climb in front. So build these sentences from your French, not from the English translation.

The forms line up too: *me, te, nos* ≍ *me, te, nous*, and third-person *le / les* ≍ *lui / leur*. Both languages also let the pronoun ride on an infinitive: *voy a llamarte* = *te voy a llamar* ≍ *je vais t'appeler*.

One Spanish habit French lacks: the indirect object is routinely doubled — *Le escribo a mi jefe*, literally "I write to him to my boss".

## J'ai parlé, he hablado {#u=A1-9}
Same shape — auxiliary + past participle — with three differences you must respect.

1. **The auxiliary is always *haber*.** Never *ser*. *He ido, he salido, me he levantado*, where French picks *être*: *je suis allé, je suis sorti, je me suis levé*.
2. **No agreement, ever.** *María ha llegado*, *las he visto* — French would write *arrivée*, *vues*. The participle after *haber* is frozen.
3. **Nothing comes between the two parts.** *He viajado siempre*, never "he siempre viajado", where French says *j'ai toujours voyagé*.

And a warning for what is coming: the Spanish *perfecto* is **not** the workhorse your *passé composé* is. It covers today, this week, and life experience. Finished, dated events need another tense — see unit A2-2.

## Depuis, ça fait… que {#u=A2-1}
Here Spanish does exactly what French does, and English cannot follow. All three of these keep the verb in the **present**, precisely as in French:

- **desde** + a starting point: *Vivo aquí desde 2019* ≍ *j'habite ici depuis 2019*
- **desde hace** + a length of time: *Vivo aquí desde hace tres años* ≍ *…depuis trois ans*
- **hace… que** + present: *Hace tres años que vivo aquí* ≍ *ça fait trois ans que j'habite ici*

English is forced into "I **have** lived here for three years", which is why English speakers struggle here and you need not. Keep the French reflex.

One separation to learn: bare *hace tres años*, with no *que*, means *il y a trois ans* — "ago" — and takes a past tense: *Llegué hace tres años* ≍ *je suis arrivé il y a trois ans*.

## Attention au passé composé {#u=A2-2}
Your *passé composé* is now a trap: it **looks** like *he hablado* but usually does the work of *hablé*. In spoken French, *j'ai parlé* covers everything finished; Spanish splits that job in two, and dated, completed events go to the **indefinido**: *Nací en 1990* ≍ *je suis né en 1990*; *Ayer comí paella* ≍ *hier, j'ai mangé une paella*. Neither would be *he nacido* or *he comido*.

The good news is that you already know these forms: *hablé, habló, hablaron* are your *passé simple* (*je parlai, il parla, ils parlèrent*) — the tense you read in novels and never speak. In Spanish it is the ordinary, spoken narrative past. In most of Latin America it takes over almost everything, even "today".

## Le gérondif espagnol {#u=A2-4}
*Estar + gerundio* ≍ *être en train de*: *estoy comiendo* ≍ *je suis en train de manger*. Use it only for what is happening right now; for a habit Spanish uses the plain present, just as French does — *trabajo en un banco*, not "estoy trabajando en un banco".

The trap is the little word *en*. French *en + gérondif* **loses its *en*** in Spanish: *en travaillant, j'ai beaucoup appris* → ***Trabajando** aprendí mucho*. "En trabajando" does not exist.

After a preposition, both languages use the infinitive, so trust yourself: *antes de salir* ≍ *avant de sortir*, *después de comer* ≍ *après avoir mangé*.

Free gift in this unit: the conditional is built as in French, infinitive + endings — *hablaría* ≍ *je parlerais*, *me gustaría* ≍ *j'aimerais*, *¿podría?* ≍ *pourriez-vous ?*

## Je le lui donne {#u=A2-6}
Both languages stack two object pronouns in front of the verb, in a fixed order that English simply cannot reproduce. But the order is **not** the same, and this is where French misleads you.

French puts the **direct** object first when the indirect one is third person: *je le lui donne*, *je la leur montre*. Spanish always puts the **indirect** first: *se lo doy*, *se la enseño*. So *le lui* → *se lo*, with the two halves swapped — and *le/les* becomes **se** in front of *lo, la, los, las* ("le lo" is impossible).

With *me, te, nos* the two languages agree: *me lo das* ≍ *tu me le donnes*. And both can hang the pair on an infinitive: *voy a dárselo* ≍ *je vais le lui donner*.

## Le subjonctif est là {#u=A2-8}
You have an advantage English speakers do not: your subjunctive is still alive — *que je sois, qu'il fasse, que tu viennes*. That is exactly what the *usted* imperative and every negative imperative are made of: *hable, coma, venga; no hables, no vengas, no se preocupe*.

Recognise the family and the forms stop looking random. Like French, Spanish builds this mood on a present-tense stem — Spanish takes the *yo* form (*pongo → ponga*), French the *ils* form (*ils mettent → que je mette*) — and the same short list of verbs goes its own way: *sea, vaya, sepa* ≍ *sois, aille, sache*.

One warning: Spanish uses this mood far more than modern French does — after *quiero que*, *espero que*, *cuando* + future — which is waiting for you at B1.

## Imparfait, sans surprise {#u=A2-9}
*Hablaba* ≍ *je parlais*, job for job. Habits: *todos los veranos íbamos a la playa* ≍ *tous les étés nous allions à la plage*. Background and description: *era de noche y llovía* ≍ *il faisait nuit et il pleuvait*. Age, weather, feelings, "what things were like" — the two tenses overlap almost completely.

It is even easier than French: only three irregular verbs, *era, iba, veía* ≍ *j'étais, j'allais, je voyais*.

And the choice this unit sets up — *imperfecto* or *indefinido* — is the choice you already make between *imparfait* and *passé simple/composé*: the scene versus the event. *Cuando llegué, mi hermana dormía* ≍ *quand je suis arrivé, ma sœur dormait*. Trust that instinct. It transfers.
