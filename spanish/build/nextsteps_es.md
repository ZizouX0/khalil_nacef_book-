# Where to Go After Unit 20

A1 and A2, finished. In CEFR terms you can now handle most of everyday life in Spanish: introducing yourself,
shopping, eating out, describing your flat and your past, getting what you need out of a pharmacy, telling a
simple story.

What you cannot do is hold a long conversation about anything abstract, and fast native speech will still
arrive as a blur. That is exactly where you should be. Here is how to get past it.

## 1. Make the jump from reading Spanish to hearing it

This is where self-taught learners stall: comfortable on the page, lost the moment somebody opens their
mouth. The cure is volume of *comprehensible* input — material a little above your level, and a lot of it.

- **Slow, graded podcasts.** Anything labelled "Spanish for beginners / intermediate", where the speaker
  deliberately slows down. Listen to the same episode three times: once cold, once with the transcript, once
  cold again. The third listen is the one that teaches you.
- **Spanish subtitles, never English ones.** English subtitles let your brain skip the Spanish entirely.
  Spanish subtitles hook the sound onto the spelling.
- **Shadowing, ten minutes a day.** Play a line, pause, say it back at the same speed and with the same tune.
  Then play it again and speak *over* the speaker. You are copying rhythm, not meaning. Of all the
  pronunciation techniques anyone has studied, this is the one with the most consistent evidence behind it.

## 2. Read something longer than a paragraph

- **Graded readers** (A2 / B1) — short novels written on a controlled vocabulary. The most pleasant way there
  is to consolidate everything in this book.
- **This book's own dialogues**, a month from now. They will feel absurdly easy, and that feeling is worth
  more than a test score.
- **Children's news sites** and short-article sites. Real Spanish, short enough to finish.

## 3. Turn your own glossary into a review deck

The *Verb & Adjective Glossary* and the *Alphabetical Index* at the back are already a vocabulary list
tailored to exactly what you have studied. Put them into a spaced-repetition app (Anki is the best known, and
free on desktop), with two rules:

- **Never make a card for a word you have not met in a sentence.** Cards built from bare word lists are the
  main reason people quit Anki in week three.
- **Put the whole phrase on the card, not the single word.** *la cuenta* is hard. *¿Me trae la cuenta, por
  favor?* is easy, and it teaches you the word anyway.

## 4. Speak to a person

Nothing in this book replaces this, and it's the step almost everyone postpones for years. I postponed it for
two. Don't.

- **A tutor** on a language-exchange or tutoring platform costs less than you think, and one hour a week
  changes everything. For a first lesson, say this: *"Hola. Acabo de terminar un curso de nivel A2. Quiero
  practicar la conversación. ¿Podemos hablar de cosas cotidianas y me corriges los errores importantes?"*
- **A language exchange.** An hour of English for an hour of Spanish. Free, and better than anything else at
  killing the fear of opening your mouth.

## 5. If you want a certificate

The **DELE** diplomas from the Instituto Cervantes are the recognised international qualification, and they
never expire. **DELE A2** is the exam this book has prepared you for. Four parts — reading (60 min),
listening (40 min), writing (45–50 min) and speaking (12 min) — and you need 30/50 in each of the two groups
to pass. Scoring 40+ on the A2 tests here means you are close. Add listening practice and one or two timed
rehearsals.

## What B1 looks like, so you know what you're aiming at

At B1 you cope with most of what travelling throws at you, describe experiences and ambitions, give reasons
for your opinions, and follow the main points of a clear standard discussion. The big grammar you have not
met yet is the **subjunctive**, plus the future tense and the compound past tenses. After that it is
vocabulary and hours.

> **The honest estimate.** The Instituto Cervantes puts A1 at roughly 60 guided hours and A2 at roughly
> 120–150 hours in total. B1 is another 150–200 on top. If that sounds like a lot, add up what you have
> already put into this book — and notice that you did it anyway.
