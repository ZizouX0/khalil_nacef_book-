# Where to Go After Unit 20

You have finished A1 and A2. In CEFR terms that means you can handle most everyday situations: introducing
yourself, shopping, eating out, describing your home and your past, dealing with a pharmacy, telling a
simple story. What you cannot yet do is hold a long conversation about anything abstract, and you will still
find fast native speech hard. That is completely normal at this point, and here is how to move past it.

## 1. Make the jump from reading Spanish to hearing it

This is where most self-taught learners stall: they can read a page comfortably and understand almost
nothing spoken. The cure is volume of *comprehensible* input — material just slightly above your level.

- **Slow, graded podcasts.** Look for anything labelled "Spanish for beginners / intermediate" where the
  speaker deliberately slows down. Listen to the same episode three times: once cold, once with the
  transcript, once cold again. The third listen is the one that teaches you.
- **Watch with Spanish subtitles, never English ones.** English subtitles let your brain skip the Spanish
  entirely. Spanish subtitles force you to connect sound to spelling.
- **Shadowing, ten minutes a day.** Play a line, pause, and say it back at the same speed and with the same
  tune. Then play it again and speak *over* the speaker. You are copying rhythm, not meaning. Of all the
  pronunciation techniques studied, this is the one with the most consistent evidence behind it.

## 2. Read something longer than a paragraph

- **Graded readers** (A2 / B1 level) — short novels written with a controlled vocabulary. This is the single
  most pleasant way to consolidate everything in this book.
- **Re-read this book's dialogues** a month from now. You will be surprised how easy they have become, and
  that feeling is worth more than a test score.
- **Children's news sites and short-article sites in Spanish** — real Spanish, short enough to finish.

## 3. Turn your own glossary into a review deck

The *Verb & Adjective Glossary* and the *Alphabetical Index* at the back of this book are already a
vocabulary list tailored to what you have studied. Put them into a spaced-repetition app (Anki is the
best-known and free on desktop) — but follow two rules:

- **Never make a card for a word you have not met in a sentence.** Cards made from bare word lists are the
  main reason people quit Anki.
- **Put the whole phrase on the card, not the single word.** *la cuenta* is hard; *¿Me trae la cuenta, por
  favor?* is easy, and it teaches you the word anyway.

## 4. Speak to a person

Nothing in this book replaces this, and it is the step most people postpone for years.

- **A tutor** on a language-exchange or tutoring platform costs less than you think and one hour a week
  changes everything. For a first lesson, say this: *"Hola. Acabo de terminar un curso de nivel A2. Quiero
  practicar la conversación. ¿Podemos hablar de cosas cotidianas y me corriges los errores importantes?"*
- **A language exchange** — you give an hour of English, you get an hour of Spanish. Free, and it removes
  the fear of speaking faster than anything else.

## 5. If you want a certificate

The **DELE** diplomas from the Instituto Cervantes are the recognised international qualification, and they
never expire. **DELE A2** is the exam this book has prepared you for. It has four parts — reading (60 min),
listening (40 min), writing (45–50 min) and speaking (12 min) — and you need 30/50 in each of the two
groups to pass. If you have been scoring 40+ on the A2 tests in this book, you are close to ready; what you
will need to add is listening practice and one or two timed rehearsals.

## What B1 looks like, so you know what you're aiming at

At B1 you can handle most situations that come up while travelling, describe experiences and ambitions,
give reasons and explanations for your opinions, and follow the main points of a clear standard discussion.
The main grammar you have not met yet is the **subjunctive**, plus the future tense and the compound past
tenses. Everything else is vocabulary and hours.

> **The honest estimate.** The Instituto Cervantes puts A1 at roughly 60 guided hours and A2 at roughly
> 120–150 hours in total. B1 is another 150–200 on top. If that sounds like a lot, count what you have
> already put in to finish this book — and notice you did it anyway.
