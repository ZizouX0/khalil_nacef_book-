# How to play

This is the third book of the course, and the only one you cannot use alone. The first book teaches
you, the second makes it stick, this one makes you **say it to another person** — which is the one
thing neither of the others can do.

You need a friend at roughly your level and twenty minutes. No teacher, no preparation, no props.

## The one rule

**Each of you wants something, and the other one is in the way.**

That is the whole design. Every scene in this book is a version of it.

It exists because of how role-plays normally fail. *"You are in a restaurant, order a meal"* produces
four lines and an awkward silence — nothing is at stake, nobody has to work for anything, and you both
know it is pretend. Give the waiter an empty kitchen and give the customer fifteen euros and a rule
about meat, and neither of you can finish quickly. You stop performing Spanish and start using it.

## Setting up a scene

1. **Find the scene for the unit you have just finished.** Both of you read the *Situación*, the
   *Termina cuando*, the *Reto extra* and the *Para hablar* list.
2. **Now read only your own box** — *Papel A* or *Papel B*, never both. Crease the page down the
   dashed line if you do not trust yourselves. Not knowing what the other one wants is the whole
   point; if you read both boxes you have read the ending.
3. **Talk.** Four to six minutes. Stop when the *Termina cuando* condition is met.
4. **Then, and only then, read the model at the back.**

## Scoring

Keep it on paper if you like, or just keep it in your head.

- **You score together, never against each other.** One target per scene, and you both hit it or you
  both miss.
- **A word of English costs a point.** Gestures are free. *¿Cómo se dice…?* is free. Drawing on a
  napkin is free.
- **The Reto extra is a bonus point**, not a requirement.

Scoring together is not politeness, it is the thing that makes the book work. If you competed,
whichever of you is stronger would win every scene and the other would gradually stop talking — which
is precisely the outcome this book exists to prevent. Because you share the score, helping your friend
find a word is the winning move rather than a favour.

## The three kinds of scene

Every unit has one of each, so you are never doing the same thing three times in an evening.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Transacción** | One of you wants a thing or a service; the other controls it | shops, restaurants, chemists, reception desks, interviews |
| **Negociación** | You want incompatible things and must reach **one** agreement | flatmates, holidays, splitting a bill, whose turn it is |
| **Social** | Meeting, explaining, telling, apologising — nothing changes hands | parties, first days, running late, bad news |

If one of the three feels much harder than the others, that is worth knowing. Most learners find
**Negociación** hardest, because you cannot prepare a negotiation in advance — you have to answer
what the other person actually said.

## The model conversations

They are at the back, and they are **not** the right answer. There is no right answer; two people
playing the same scene twice will not produce the same conversation.

Their job is different. At this level the problem is usually not that you said something wrong — it is
that you did not know there was a better thing to say. A model read straight after you have struggled
shows you that thing. The same model read beforehand turns the scene into a script and wastes it.

So: play first. Always.

## What to do with the second Después question

Every scene ends by asking **what is one thing you wanted to say and could not.** Write it down
somewhere — the back of this book, your phone, anywhere.

That list is the most valuable thing you will produce with this book, and it is worth more than the
score. It is a record of exactly what your Spanish is missing, arrived at by trying to use it rather
than by guessing. Look it up afterwards, and you will remember it, because you already wanted it.

## How often

**One session per unit**, after you have finished the unit and done its workbook chapter. Three scenes
is about twenty minutes. Playing the same three scenes again a week later is not a waste — you will
say different things, and the second time is where the fluency comes from.

## One honest warning

Two learners at the same level cannot correct each other reliably. You will both say things that are
wrong, and agree that they were fine.

That is a real cost and it is worth naming. It is also the wrong reason not to do this. Speaking
inaccurately is how everybody learns to speak; the alternative on offer is not speaking accurately,
it is not speaking at all. Use the models to catch the big things, take the *Para hablar* phrases as
correct because they come from the course book, and accept that some errors will slip through.

Fluency and accuracy are built separately. This book is for the first one.
